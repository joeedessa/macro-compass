1. Three timeframes, three questions

Every moving average here is a simple average of closes over a fixed number of bars, and the bar is what changes between timeframes — not the arithmetic.

TimeframeOne bar isBar boundaryHistory fetchedQuestion it answers
Dailyone session's closeeach trading day2 years (~500 bars) Where is price against the swing-trading lines. Entries and stops live here.
WeeklyFriday's close for the weekMonday 00:00 UTC5 years (~262 bars) The intermediate trend. Whether a daily move is a dip inside a trend or the start of a break.
Monthlythe last close of the monththe 1st, 00:00 UTC25 years (~300 bars) The primary trend. Whether the intermediate trend is a phase or a regime.

The windows are deliberate. Yahoo honours the interval on a bounded range but silently ignores it on range=max, returning the same ~168 downsampled bars whatever you ask for — so the weekly and monthly series are requested at exactly 5 and 25 years.

2. The lengths, and why they differ by timeframe

The daily set does not transplant onto slower bars. A 200-period average on daily bars is forty weeks; on monthly bars it is seventeen years, which is economic history rather than a trend line. Each timeframe uses the lengths practitioners actually read on it.

TimeframeLengthsWhat each one is
Daily5 · 21 · 50 · 150 · 200 5 a week · 21 a month, the fast swing line · 50 the swing trader's trend line · 150 and 200 the levels treated as support and resistance. The 200 is the one the whole industry watches.
Weekly10 · 30 · 40 · 200 10 ~2 months, the fast weekly line · 30 Weinstein's stage line, the backbone of stage analysis · 40 the 200-day expressed in weeks (200 ÷ 5) · 200 ~4 years, the cycle floor, heavily used in crypto and long-cycle assets.
Monthly10 · 12 · 20 10 the 200-day expressed in months and the length Faber's tactical work is built on · 12 one year · 20 ~two years. Nothing longer: a 50-month is four years and no longer describes a trend anyone trades.

These are the same everywhere on the site. The trend map settled them first; the toggle columns on every other page and the portfolio pipeline were then brought into line, so a "weekly 30" means the same thing on the Watchlist as it does on Countries.

The one thing to internalise: the three timeframes are not three opinions about the same trend, they are three different trends. A name can be below its daily 50, above its 30-week and above its 10-month at the same time — and that specific combination is a pullback in an uptrend, the swing trader's setup, not a contradiction. Reading them together is the point, and section 7 does exactly that.

4. Above or below: how a cell reads

Every moving-average cell on the site says three things: ▲ or ▼ for which side of the average price is on, the distance as a percentage, and on the slow lines a level tag when the average is being tested. The caret is there because a signed percentage in a table full of signed percentages does not read as a position — you would have to know that plus means above, and the returns columns beside it use the same sign for something else entirely.

Distance is close / average − 1. Hover any cell for the average's actual level.

5. The level test — held · rejected · reclaimed · lost

Shown only on the lines a chartist treats as support and resistance: daily 150 and 200, weekly 30, 40 and 200, monthly 10 and 20. Tagging every column would bury them.

  • held — price came down to the line from above and stayed above it
  • rejected — price came up to the line from below and turned back down
  • reclaimed — price crossed the line from below to above
  • lost — price crossed the line from above to below

Two rules make the tag mean something. The tolerance for "near" is scaled to the instrument — half its typical bar-to-bar move, floored at 1% and capped at 4% — so a quiet bond fund and a volatility index each get a "test" that means something for them. And the tag stays only while price is still near the line — within three times that tolerance. Beyond that the position is simply above or below, and the caret already says so.

Both rules exist because the first version, a fixed 2% held for fifteen bars, put "rejected" beside VIX at −26%: technically true, since VIX had come within 1.9% four weeks earlier, and useless, since VIX moves twenty per cent in a week.

6. Crosses

A cross is a sign change in fast − slow between two consecutive bars. Two ladders exist and they answer different questions.

The scan ladder (Markets, Themes, Countries, Watchlist, Lookup)

Five tiers, fast to slow, on whichever timeframe is selected. Read left to right as escalation: each step costs time and buys confidence, and price leads its own averages arithmetically, so a name normally lights up on the left first and works rightward.

TierLegsReads as
5 × 21EMA 5 vs EMA 21timing trigger — earliest and noisiest
price × 50close vs SMA 50swing trend break
21 × 50EMA 21 vs SMA 50intermediate turn
price × 200close vs SMA 200regime change
50 × 200SMA 50 vs SMA 200golden / death cross — latest and most reliable

Note the 5 and 21 in this ladder are exponential; the 5 and 21 columns elsewhere are simple. That split predates the review and the columns were left simple so no figure anyone had been reading moved. Every page that shows both says so.

Major crosses (Trend map)

Only the crosses a chartist would mark on the chart, on the chartist lengths — weekly: price × 30w, price × 40w, 10w × 40w, price × 200w · monthly: price × 10mo, price × 20mo, 10mo × 20mo — over short windows (last 4 weeks, last 3 months), and only crosses that held. A cross that reversed inside the window is chop, not a change of trend; those are counted in the heading rather than listed, so you can see how much was filtered.

Why: a first pass over a full year turned up 226 price-through-10-month crosses across 142 names, more than half of them whipsawed. Arithmetically right, useless to read.

7. The alignment read — three timeframes, one sentence

On the trend map. Each instrument sits above or below its daily 50, its 30-week and its 10-month, and the eight combinations are the stages a trend passes through. The lines are fixed — this is a statement of fact about the instrument and it does not move when the map's period buttons are pressed.

d · w · mStateWhat it is, and what a trader typically does there
111Full uptrendevery timeframe agrees. Buy dips, do not chase — the daily is where the entry is.
011Pullback in uptrendthe long trend is intact and price is resting inside it. The swing trader's setup: the dip to buy, with the 30-week as the stop.
001Deep pullbackthe intermediate trend has broken while the primary holds. Either the buy of the cycle or the first leg of a top — the 10-month decides.
101Bounce inside a weekly breaka short-term bounce inside a broken intermediate trend. Not a base until the 30-week is reclaimed.
110Recoveryturning up before the primary trend has. Where new uptrends come from — and where bear-market rallies die.
010Early turnan intermediate uptrend fighting a primary downtrend, with the daily already fading. Fragile.
100Bounce in downtrenda short-term bounce inside a broken trend on both higher timeframes. Sell the rally, or wait for the 30-week.
000Full downtrendevery timeframe agrees. Do not buy the dip; the first thing to reclaim is the daily 50.

The states are descriptions of where price sits, not forecasts. The sentence says what a trader typically does there; the trader still decides.

9. Data rules that took a bug to learn

  • The phantom bar. Yahoo pads a trailing bar onto every weekly and monthly series, stamped with the last trade and repeating the close of the period in progress. Left in, the current week or month is counted twice in every average — biased toward the latest price by up to 1.4 points on a 10-month, always in the flattering direction. Real bars sit on the period boundary (Monday, the 1st); the phantom does not. It is dropped at the source, client and server, when it is off-boundary and repeats the prior close. Zero false positives over 561 historical bars.
  • Live tick versus settled close. On the daily set the last bar is the running session while the market is open, so a name near a line can sit either side of it intraday and settle on the other. Only the Watchlist acts on settled closes; every other daily read is the live tick, and the note under the toggles says so.
  • Delisted instruments do not leave the feed. Yahoo keeps serving old history and pads a null bar at today's timestamp, so the last row looks current. Staleness is measured from the last non-null close, and anything more than ten days old is dropped and named.
  • Symbols can resolve with no usable history. Depth is checked, not just existence. Anything without enough bars for the average reads as a dash, never as zero.
  • Native currency, always. Prices are never converted. The one exception is a column that ranks the whole universe against itself, which is converted once and labelled (USD).
